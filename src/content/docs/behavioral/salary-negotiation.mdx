## Why it matters in interviews

Compensation is discussed early (often in the very first recruiter call) and again at the offer stage. A poorly handled answer can cost you more than any technical mistake, because your first number anchors everything after it. The recruiter is assessing:

- **Are you within budget** for the band this role is approved for?
- **Are you realistic?** A well-reasoned expectation signals maturity; a random number signals the opposite.
- **Will you join?** Notice period, other offers and counter-offer risk all affect whether they make an offer.

Negotiation is normal and expected at both service and product companies. Done politely and with reasons, it does not put an offer at risk.

## Step 1: Research your range

Build a range **before** the first recruiter call, not during it.

| Source | What it gives you |
|---|---|
| Salary sites (e.g. AmbitionBox, Glassdoor, Levels.fyi for product companies) | Ranges by company, role and experience. Treat as rough; filter for your level and city |
| Peers and ex-colleagues at the target company or similar ones | The most accurate data. Ask about band, variable payout and hike patterns |
| Recruiters (third-party) | Market ranges for your profile; ask them directly |
| Your own offers and interview processes | Real market data points |
| Company type and tier | Service, product, GCC (global capability centre), startup and MNC bands differ widely |

Write down three numbers: your **current total compensation** (fixed and variable split), your **target** and your **walk-away** number. Know your current CTC breakdown exactly: fixed, variable, employer PF, gratuity, insurance, any retention bonus. Recruiters often ask for payslips later, so be accurate.

## Step 2: Answering "What is your expected CTC?"

The usual approaches, from best to fallback:

1. **Defer politely** until you know the role better.
2. **Give a researched range** whose lower end you would be happy with.
3. **Anchor on the role, not your current pay**, especially if you are underpaid.

```text
DEFER
"I'd like to understand the role and the level first. I'm sure that if I'm the
right fit, we'll be able to agree on a number. Could you share the budgeted
range for this position?"

RANGE (if pressed)
"Based on my research for [role/level] in [city] at [company type], and the
scope of this role, I'm looking for a fixed component in the range of
[lower]-[upper]. I'm flexible depending on the overall structure:
variable, ESOPs and benefits."

CURRENT CTC (if asked directly)
"My current CTC is [X], of which [Y] is fixed and [Z] is variable.
My expectation is based on the role and the market rather than a percentage
hike on that."
```

Indian service companies often apply a percentage hike on current CTC and may have firm caps. Product companies and GCCs tend to price the role and level. Know which you are dealing with.

<Aside type="tip" title="Sample: replace with your own numbers and reasoning">
"My current CTC is [X], with about 90% fixed. For an SDE-2 role at a product company in Bengaluru, my research suggests a fixed range of [A]-[B], and that's what I'm targeting. I'm open to discussing how the variable and ESOP components fit in."
</Aside>

## Step 3: Negotiating the offer

1. **Get the offer in writing** with the full breakup before negotiating.
2. **Thank them and express interest.** Never accept or reject on the call; ask for a day or two.
3. **Make one clear, reasoned ask.** Tie it to data: market range, a competing offer, your scope.
4. **Negotiate the total, not just base:** joining bonus, notice buyout, level/title, relocation, ESOPs, start date.
5. **Stay polite and specific.** Recruiters can only push internally for something concrete.

```text
"Thank you, I'm genuinely excited about the role and the team. Having looked at the
breakup, the fixed component is below what I was expecting given [market data /
competing offer of X / the scope we discussed]. Would it be possible to bring the
fixed component to [number]? If the band is fixed, a joining bonus of [amount]
to cover [notice buyout / variable I'll lose] would help close the gap."
```

## Notice period and buyout

Notice periods of 60-90 days are common in India, especially at service companies, and they affect offers more than candidates expect.

| Topic | Practical advice |
|---|---|
| Stating your notice period | Be exact: "90 days, negotiable to [N] with my manager" or "buyout possible". Do not understate it; offers have been revoked over this |
| Serving notice before an offer | Gives you a faster joining date and more interest from recruiters, but adds financial and negotiation risk. Decide based on your savings and market demand |
| Buyout | Some employers allow paying for unserved notice days (usually calculated on basic or gross pay). Some new employers reimburse it as a joining bonus; ask explicitly |
| Early release | Often possible if you hand over well and your manager agrees. Start the conversation politely right after resigning |
| Leave balance | Some companies let you adjust earned leave against notice. Check your policy |
| Multiple offers during notice | Common in India. Be honest with recruiters if they ask; reneging on a signed offer can affect your reputation with that company and recruiter |
| Counter-offers | If you are leaving for reasons beyond money, a counter-offer rarely fixes them. Decide your stance before resigning |

Always get the **relieving letter and experience letter** process clear before your last day, and check your appointment letter for any clause about notice, bond or non-compete.

## Evaluating an offer

Compare offers on the same basis. The CTC number on the offer letter is not what lands in your bank account.

| Component | What to check |
|---|---|
| **Fixed / base** | The most important number. Hikes and future offers are usually based on it |
| **Variable / performance bonus** | Target percentage, how it is calculated (individual vs company performance), and the **actual average payout** in recent years |
| **Joining bonus** | One-time; often has a clawback if you leave within 12 months |
| **ESOPs / RSUs** | RSUs (listed companies) have a clear value. ESOPs (startups) need: number of options, strike price, vesting schedule and cliff, exercise window after leaving, last valuation. Value them conservatively |
| **Retention bonus** | Paid after a period; check conditions |
| **Employer PF and gratuity** | Included in CTC but not take-home. Gratuity only after the eligibility period |
| **Insurance** | Health cover amount, whether family and parents are covered, top-up options |
| **Other benefits** | Meal cards, learning budget, internet/WFH allowance, relocation support |
| **Work arrangement** | Remote, hybrid or office; commute time has a real cost |
| **Level and title** | Affects your next job's starting point and your next promotion timeline |
| **Non-financial** | Team, tech stack, learning, product, manager, stability, brand |

## Do / Don't

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Research a range before the first call | Make up a number on the spot |
| Know your exact current CTC breakup | Inflate current CTC; payslips are often checked |
| Negotiate politely with reasons | Give ultimatums or bluff about offers you do not have |
| Ask for the full offer breakup in writing | Accept or decline on the call |
| Compare fixed pay and real take-home | Compare headline CTC numbers |
| Be honest about notice period | Understate notice and hope to sort it out later |

## Pitfalls

<Aside type="caution" title="Common mistakes">
- **Anchoring too low.** Your first number is usually the ceiling. Research first.
- **Hike-percentage thinking only.** If you are underpaid, a percentage on current pay keeps you underpaid. Anchor on the role and market.
- **Counting variable, ESOPs or one-time bonuses as guaranteed income.**
- **Faking offers or payslips.** Background verification checks documents; offers get revoked.
- **Accepting multiple offers and dropping out at the last minute** without telling anyone. Communicate early and honestly.
- **Ignoring the clawback** on a joining bonus or relocation support.
</Aside>

## Follow-up questions to expect

<details>
<summary>"This is above our budget. Can you be flexible?"</summary>

Ask what the band is, then decide. If the role is a great fit, you can trade: a lower fixed for a joining bonus, an earlier review, a higher level, or more ESOPs. Do not drop to your walk-away number in one step.
</details>

<details>
<summary>"Do you have any other offers?"</summary>

Be truthful. If yes, share the company type and the fact of the offer, and the number only if it helps you. If no, say you are in process with others, if true. Never invent an offer.
</details>

<details>
<summary>"Why are you asking for such a big hike?"</summary>

Explain with data: market range for the role, increased scope since your last revision, and the responsibilities in this role. Keep it about the role, not your personal expenses.
</details>

<details>
<summary>"Can you join in 30 days?"</summary>

Answer honestly: "My notice is [N] days. I'll request early release and I can do a buyout if needed. Realistically I can join by [date]." Offer a firm date you can meet.
</details>
